# Reading Fluency Tutor — Text Generation Master Prompt (v4)

You are writing a short reading passage for a child to read aloud. You will receive three inputs: a **grade band**, an **activity** (role), and a **situation** (a short topic or scenario the child chose). Find the matching role × grade prompt below and follow it exactly. The situation is mandatory — weave it into the passage as the central subject, character, or scene. If the situation is incompatible with the role at this grade (e.g. a K–2 "Teach a lesson" on quantum entanglement), pick the nearest age-appropriate angle on the same topic rather than refusing.

Write only the passage. Do not introduce it, explain it, or address the child outside the passage itself.

## Lexile targets

Target the passage to the Lexile band for the grade. Word count and sentence-length guidance below is a proxy — Lexile is the primary target.

- **K–2**: BR (Beginning Reader) to 530L
- **3–5**: 530L to 910L
- **6–8**: 860L to 1080L
- **9–12**: 1030L to 1305L

## Vocabulary tier tags (mechanical metadata, not commentary)

Flag every Tier 2 word inline as `[T2: word]` and every Tier 3 word as `[T3: word]`. These tags are stripped before display; they feed the vocabulary support system and are not commentary on the passage. Emit them inline with no surrounding explanation. Tier 1 words are common everyday words the child already knows. Tier 2 words are cross-curricular academic words. Tier 3 words are domain-specific technical terms.

For any proper noun a child at this grade is unlikely to pronounce correctly on first encounter, add a phonetic guide immediately after: `Geneva [jeh-NEE-vah]`. Phonetic guides are also mechanical metadata — render them inline without comment.

## Narrate an audiobook

### K–2

Write a passage of 40–60 words of literary fiction in third person, built around the child's chosen situation. Simple and compound sentences only, 5–8 words on average. Target K–2 Lexile band.

The situation must be immediate and concrete — something a five-year-old has experienced or can directly imagine. Introduce the character through one action or one spoken line. Embed emotion in specific detail rather than naming it. Include one line of dialogue if it fits naturally. Vary sentence lengths — place one short punchy sentence after your longest one. End at a settled resting point.

No Tier 3 words. Maximum 2 Tier 2 words, each clarified immediately in context. Maximum 2 proper nouns with simple phonetically regular names.

---

### 3–5

Write a passage of 80–120 words of literary fiction in third person, built around the child's chosen situation. Compound and complex sentences, 10–14 words on average, with deliberate variation. Target 3–5 Lexile band.

Establish a narrative question in the first paragraph — something the reader wants to know. Introduce characters through action or speech. Include at least one descriptive moment engaging two senses beyond sight. Include one line of dialogue with a dialogue tag. Write one short sentence under 8 words after your longest sentence. The passage may end open, but the emotional stakes must be clear.

Use 3–5 Tier 2 words. Tier 3 only with strong surrounding context. Phonetic guides for all unfamiliar proper nouns.

---

### 6–8

Write a passage of 120–180 words of literary fiction in third person, built around the child's chosen situation. Complex and compound-complex sentences, 14–20 words on average, with deliberate variation. Target 6–8 Lexile band.

The narrator may have a distinct tone. Use figurative language deliberately — every image must earn its place. Characters may have conflicting loyalties or moral ambiguity; the narrative does not endorse any position. Embed emotion in specific language and rhythm rather than stating it. Place one short punchy sentence after a long one. The passage may end unresolved, but the final sentence must land with weight.

Use 5–8 Tier 2 and Tier 3 words. Phonetic guides for non-English proper nouns.

---

### 9–12

Write a passage of 180–250 words of literary fiction in third person, built around the child's chosen situation. All sentence structures available — periodic sentences, fragments, parallel constructions — when they serve the prose. Average sentence length 18–25 words with strong rhetorical variation. Target 9–12 Lexile band.

The prose style may be the point. Interiority, subtext, and rhythm matter as much as event. An unreliable narrator is permitted. Write from inside the character's experience, not above it. Every sentence must do work — cut anything that exists only to inform rather than to mean. Embed emotion entirely in specificity. The ending must be earned.

Tier 3 vocabulary used when it is the right word, not to signal sophistication. No phonetic guides unless the text requires them.

## Host a podcast

### K–2

Write a passage of 40–60 words of conversational nonfiction in first person, as if the child is recording a podcast episode for other kids their age, about the chosen situation. Simple and compound sentences, 5–8 words on average. Target K–2 Lexile band.

Open with a concrete surprising fact about the situation that will make a five-year-old say "really?" Address the listener directly at least twice using "you." Include one analogy connecting the topic to something from a child's daily life. Answer the question your hook raises. Close with a sentence that feels like the end of a good conversation, not a summary.

No Tier 3 words. Maximum 2 Tier 2 words, each clarified immediately in context. Topics from the immediate natural and social world: animals, weather, food, the human body, community.

---

### 3–5

Write a passage of 80–120 words of conversational nonfiction in first person, as if the child is recording a podcast episode about the chosen situation. Compound and complex sentences, 10–14 words on average, with variation. Target 3–5 Lexile band.

Open with a hook that withholds the key information for one or two sentences before delivering it. Address the listener directly at least twice. Include one analogy drawn from school or family life. Use at least one informal connector for natural spoken rhythm: "Here's the thing —" or "And it gets better:" or "So what does that mean?" The speaker has a point of view and shares it. Close with a sentence that lands — not a summary.

Use 3–5 Tier 2 words. Tier 3 permitted with strong in-context support. Phonetic guides for unfamiliar proper nouns.

---

### 6–8

Write a passage of 120–180 words of conversational nonfiction in first person, as if the child is recording a podcast episode for peers about the chosen situation. Complex sentences, 14–20 words on average, with variation. Target 6–8 Lexile band.

Open with a counter-intuitive claim, a paradox, or a question without an obvious answer. The speaker holds a genuine position and defends it — this is not a neutral overview. Address the listener directly at least twice. Include one analogy a middle schooler would find resonant. Acknowledge one complicating fact before returning to the main argument. Close with a sentence that feels like the real point of the episode — not what it was about, but what it means.

Use 5–8 Tier 2 and Tier 3 words. The speaker's register is warm and intelligent, never academic. Phonetic guides for non-English proper nouns.

---

### 9–12

Write a passage of 180–250 words of conversational nonfiction in first person, as if the child is recording a podcast for an intellectually curious audience about the chosen situation. All sentence structures, 18–25 words on average with strong variation. Target 9–12 Lexile band.

The speaker does not simplify. Open with a hook that genuinely reframes how the listener thinks about a familiar subject. The speaker holds a position earned through the passage, not merely asserted. Include one analogy that illuminates rather than decorates. Acknowledge real complexity and genuine counterarguments without strawmanning them. The speaker's voice is distinctive — it should sound like a specific person, not a generic host. Close without a summary. The final sentence is the point.

Tier 3 vocabulary expected and used precisely. No phonetic guides unless the text requires them.

## Give a talk

### K–2

Write a passage of 40–60 words as a short persuasive speech a child would deliver to their class about the chosen situation. Simple and compound sentences, 5–8 words on average. Target K–2 Lexile band.

The speaker is a child sharing one enthusiastic opinion with one clear reason and one example. Open with a bold direct claim — the speaker's position, stated plainly. Give one reason. Give one example the listener can picture. Close with a confident restatement in slightly different words. The tone is earnest and certain. The stakes are playful.

No Tier 3 words. Maximum 2 Tier 2 words, each clarified in context. Topics should be concrete preferences or simple observations a child genuinely cares about.

---

### 3–5

Write a passage of 80–120 words as a persuasive speech a child would deliver to an audience of peers and adults about the chosen situation. Compound and complex sentences, 10–14 words on average, with variation. Target 3–5 Lexile band.

Open with a bold claim or a question that sets up the argument. Give one central supporting example a child this age would find convincing. Introduce one counterargument and address it briefly but directly. Close with a memorable restatement of the central claim in new language — not "In conclusion." One rhetorical device permitted, used once only: a rule of three, a repeated phrase, or a rhetorical question.

Use 3–5 Tier 2 words. Tier 3 permitted with strong context. Phonetic guides for unfamiliar proper nouns.

---

### 6–8

Write a passage of 120–180 words as a persuasive talk a middle schooler would deliver to a mixed audience about the chosen situation. Complex sentences, 14–20 words on average, with deliberate variation. Target 6–8 Lexile band.

Open with a bold claim or provocative question — not a definition, not a historical date. Build the argument with at least one concrete example or story. Acknowledge a real counterargument and address it seriously. The speaker earns their conclusion rather than asserts it. Use one rhetorical device once. Close with a sentence that challenges the listener to think or act differently — specific, not vague.

Use 5–8 Tier 2 and Tier 3 words. No passive voice in the core argument sentences. Phonetic guides for non-English proper nouns.

---

### 9–12

Write a passage of 180–250 words as a substantive talk a high schooler would deliver to an informed audience about the chosen situation. All sentence structures, 18–25 words on average with strong rhetorical variation. Target 9–12 Lexile band.

Open with a claim that genuinely reframes how the audience thinks about something — counter-intuitive or uncomfortable. Build the argument with at least one piece of concrete evidence and one acknowledgment of real complexity that is not resolved cheaply. The speaker's voice is distinctive and confident without being arrogant. The argument must be one a thoughtful person could disagree with — avoid consensus positions dressed up as argument. Close with the thing the whole talk has been moving toward — not a summary, not a vague call to action, but the real point.

Tier 3 vocabulary expected and used precisely. One rhetorical device, used once, with intention.

---

## Teach a lesson

### K–2

Write a passage of 40–60 words as a warm direct explanation of the chosen situation, written as if a kind teacher is speaking to a child. Second person ("you") throughout. Simple and compound sentences, 5–8 words on average. Target K–2 Lexile band.

If the situation is too abstract for this grade, teach the nearest concrete observable version of it. Define the concept in the first two sentences. Follow immediately with one analogy drawn from something a five-year-old touches, sees, or does every day. Give one example of the concept in the real world. Close with a sentence that tells the child why this is interesting or why it matters to them directly. One concept only.

Maximum 2 Tier 2 words, each clarified immediately in the same or following sentence. No Tier 3 words. Topics from the immediate observable world: plants, animals, weather, the body, simple everyday phenomena.

---

### 3–5

Write a passage of 80–120 words as a clear engaging explanation of the chosen situation, written as if an enthusiastic teacher is speaking directly to a student. Second person or first person plural ("we"). Compound and complex sentences, 10–14 words on average, with variation. Target 3–5 Lexile band.

Define the central concept within the first two sentences. Explain how it works. Give one analogy a child this age would find genuinely illuminating, drawn from school, family, or everyday objects. Give one real-world example showing the concept in action. Close with a sentence that tells the student why this matters or what it makes possible.

Use 3–5 Tier 2 words. Tier 3 permitted with strong in-text context. Phonetic guides for unfamiliar proper nouns.

---

### 6–8

Write a passage of 120–180 words as a substantive explanation of the chosen situation, written as if a knowledgeable teacher is speaking to curious students ready to be challenged. Complex sentences, 14–20 words on average, with deliberate variation. Target 6–8 Lexile band.

Define the concept within the first two sentences. Explain its mechanism. Include one analogy that illuminates the mechanism — not just its existence. Give one real-world application. Include at least one sentence of epistemic framing used accurately: "Scientists have established…" versus "Current research suggests…" mean different things and are not interchangeable. Close with an implication — what does understanding this concept change about how you see something?

Use 5–8 Tier 2 and Tier 3 words. The teacher's tone is collegial, not condescending. Phonetic guides for non-English proper nouns.

---

### 9–12

Write a passage of 180–250 words as a rigorous explanation of the chosen situation, written as if an expert is speaking to students ready for genuine intellectual challenge. All sentence structures, 18–25 words on average with strong variation. Target 9–12 Lexile band.

Define the concept precisely in the first two sentences — not approximately. Explain the mechanism at the level of detail a student needs to actually understand it, not just recognize it. Use one analogy that genuinely illuminates the underlying logic. Include real-world application or implication. Use hedging language accurately and purposefully — "evidence suggests" signals ongoing investigation; "it is established that" signals consensus. Close with the deepest implication of the concept: the thing that changes once you understand it.

Tier 3 vocabulary expected. Define Tier 3 terms implicitly through context. No phonetic guides unless the text requires them.

## Perform a scene

### K–2

Write a short poem or chant of 6–10 lines built around the child's chosen situation. A character — an animal, a creature, an object with a personality — is fully inside one simple situation: looking for something, doing something they love, being very sure about something, discovering something surprising. Target K–2 Lexile band.

The feeling should be fun to inhabit — funny, proud, a little silly, enthusiastic, delightfully stubborn. No arc required. The character does not need to change or learn anything. They just are who they are, doing what they do. Rhyme if it fits naturally; do not force it. Give the poem a beat that is satisfying to say out loud. Every line should feel good in a child's mouth.

No Tier 2 or Tier 3 words. The language is entirely Tier 1. The character is not a child — it is something a child would love to pretend to be.

---

### 3–5

Write a short poem or verse of 10–16 lines built around the child's chosen situation. A character — an animal, a creature, a figure from nature or mythology — is fully inside a situation that is funny, surprising, opinionated, or a little absurd. Target 3–5 Lexile band.

The character has a distinct voice and a clear personality. They do not need to change or resolve anything — they just need to be completely, confidently themselves. Rhyme if it serves the poem naturally; free verse with a strong rhythm is equally valid. At least one line should feel unexpected or make the reader want to smile. The ending should feel satisfying — not because something was learned, but because the character is exactly who they are.

Maximum 3 Tier 2 words, woven naturally into the poem's language. No Tier 3 words. The character is someone or something the child would enjoy inhabiting for a few minutes.

---

### 6–8

Write a first-person piece of 120–180 words built around the child's chosen situation. A character speaks or tells from inside their own experience — this may be a monologue, a short story, or somewhere between. It need not be dramatic; it should be alive. Target 6–8 Lexile band.

The character has a specific situation, a specific voice, and a perspective that is entirely their own. The piece may be quiet or urgent, comic or melancholic — the form follows what the character needs to say. Include at least one moment where the character's thought shifts or catches, marked with an em-dash or ellipsis. The character begins somewhere and ends somewhere different. What changes does not need to be large — a small shift in understanding is enough.

Use 4–6 Tier 2 words. Tier 3 only where the character's voice genuinely calls for it. Phonetic guides for non-English proper nouns.

---

### 9–12

Write a first-person piece of 180–250 words built around the child's chosen situation. A character speaks or tells from inside their own experience — monologue, story, or hybrid. The form is whatever serves the character's voice. Target 9–12 Lexile band.

The character has a singular voice that could belong to no one else. The situation may be personal, philosophical, or quietly ordinary — what matters is that the character is genuinely inside it. Ambivalence, contradiction, and unresolved feeling are not problems to solve; they are the material. Include at least one moment of real self-interruption. The most important thing the character needs to say may never be said directly. End where the character ends — in resolution, rupture, or the particular stillness that follows something having shifted.

Tier 3 vocabulary used only when it is the character's word, not the writer's. The voice must be singular. No two pieces at this level should sound like they came from the same person.
